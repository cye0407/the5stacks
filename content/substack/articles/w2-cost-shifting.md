# Big Business Pushed Sustainability Downhill

*You're not being asked to be sustainable. You're being asked to do their reporting.*

---

It's a Thursday afternoon. You're halfway through a production issue — the kind that actually matters, the kind that affects whether you ship on time. An email lands from one of your biggest customers. Subject line: "Supplier Sustainability Assessment — Response Required."

You open it.

Scope 1 emissions. Scope 2 emissions. Scope 3 estimates. Energy consumption by source. Waste by type and destination. Water usage. Material sourcing. Carbon intensity per unit of output. Forty-seven pages. Due in two weeks.

You don't have the numbers. Not really. You have utility bills somewhere. Your waste hauler sends invoices, but you've never broken them down by category. You know roughly what your energy costs — the total, not by process. As for Scope 3, you're not entirely sure what that covers.

So you do what everyone does. You estimate. You round. You pull numbers from memory and hope they're close enough. You send it back, vaguely anxious that someone might check.

Nobody checks. This time.

---

Here's what actually happened.

The largest companies in Europe made sustainability commitments. Real ones, in many cases. Carbon neutral by 2030. Net zero by 2040. Science-based targets. Board-level sign-offs. Press releases with conviction.

Then they needed to prove it.

Not just their own operations — those are the easy part when you have a 200-person sustainability department. They needed to account for their entire value chain. Every supplier, every distributor, every logistics partner. That's Scope 3. And Scope 3 is where most of the emissions actually live.

So they did what large organisations do when they need something done but don't want to pay for it. They pushed it downstream.

They wrote questionnaires. Long ones. Full of terminology that requires a sustainability degree to parse. They put your company name at the top and sent them to every supplier in their chain. Ten-dollar words for questions that boil down to: how much energy do you use? What do you throw away? Where does your water go?

Not designed for your business. Designed for theirs. Repackaged with your name on it.

---

Let's be clear about what's happening here.

The companies with the biggest sustainability budgets — the ones with dedicated teams, enterprise software, and consultants on retainer — are outsourcing the hardest part of sustainability to the companies with the smallest budgets. The actual data collection. The operational work. The hours spent digging through invoices and utility bills trying to figure out what "Scope 2 emissions by energy source" means for a business with fourteen employees and one electricity meter.

And they're calling it "supply chain engagement."

That's not partnership. That's cost-shifting.

You're not being asked to be sustainable. You're being asked to do big business's reporting for them. Fill it in. Figure it out. Or we won't choose you next time.

The sustainability questionnaire isn't an invitation to improve. It's a compliance requirement dressed up as collaboration. The company sending it has a team of twelve working on their report. You have Svenja. And Svenja has a real job to do.

---

This is the structural squeeze that nobody talks about honestly.

CSRD — the EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive — requires large companies to report on their entire value chain. That means they need data from you. Not because they care about your sustainability journey. Because their auditor needs a number in a cell, and that number comes from your operation.

The questionnaires are getting longer. They're arriving more often. And increasingly, your answers aren't just going into a file — they're being scored, ranked, and used in procurement decisions. The supplier who can answer gets the contract. The one who can't gets replaced.

That's the game now. Not whether you're sustainable. Whether you can prove it on someone else's timeline, in someone else's format, using terminology someone else chose.

---

I'm not telling you this to make you angry. I'm telling you because understanding the game changes how you play it.

When you see the questionnaire for what it is — a reporting requirement that got pushed onto your desk — two things become clear.

First: the system is unfair. The burden falls hardest on the businesses least equipped to handle it. That's worth being honest about.

Second — and this is the part that matters: the data they're asking for isn't exotic. It's not hidden behind expensive software or specialist consultants. It's sitting in your utility bills, your waste invoices, your purchase orders, and your production records. You've been generating this data for years. You just never had a reason to organise it.

How much energy do you use? Your electricity bill knows.
What do you throw away? Your waste hauler knows.
Where does your water go? Your water bill knows.
What materials come in? Your purchase orders know.

The questionnaire makes it sound like you need a department. You don't. You need your filing cabinet and a structured way to pull the numbers together.

---

The data they're asking you to report? You're already sitting on most of it.

You just never had a reason to look at it that way. Until now.
