# Five Stacks — Launch Week Content

## Substack Post #1: "Sustainability Has a Reputation Problem — And It Earned It"

**Title:** Sustainability Has a Reputation Problem — And It Earned It

**Subtitle:** How treating sustainability as an obligation turned it into a tax — and what happens when you treat it as an operating system instead.

**Body:**

Too many frameworks sold as moral obligations. Too many reports filed and forgotten. Too many leaders who quietly write it off as a tax on doing business.

I know because I spent a decade inside the problem — from EU ecolabel legislation to startup cooperatives, shop floors to senior operations management, across food, consumer goods, agriculture, and manufacturing. I built compliance systems and certification tools through Ecosystems United, helping SMEs across Europe get supplier-ready. I watched the same pattern everywhere.

The pattern is so consistent it might as well be a template.

**Step one:** Someone in leadership announces a sustainability commitment. Genuine enthusiasm. A press release goes out. The target gets added to the company website. "Carbon neutral by 2030." Real conviction.

**Step two:** It becomes a department. One person. Small budget. Lots of PowerPoints. A consultant comes in, produces a thick report full of recommendations and acronyms, charges a number that makes your finance team wince, and leaves. You never see them again.

**Step three:** It hits reality. The costs are higher than expected. The returns are invisible. Customers don't reward you for it the way the consultant said they would. Other priorities — the ones that actually show up on a P&L — become more urgent. The sustainability person starts getting fewer meeting invites.

**Step four:** Theater. The report still gets published. The language stays on the website. The commitment is technically still alive. But your operations run exactly as they did before. Nothing changed. Nothing was ever going to change.

Then the budget gets reviewed, and the sustainability line item is the first thing cut. Because why wouldn't it be? It never produced anything but a PDF.

---

I'm not describing a failure of ambition. I'm describing a failure of infrastructure.

The people who set those targets weren't faking it — most of them genuinely wanted results. The problem was never motivation. The problem was that the model they were given was designed backwards.

Old sustainability starts with the destination: a target, a certification, a net-zero pledge. Then it tries to figure out how to get there. This is like announcing you'll run a marathon and then wondering where to buy running shoes. The ambition is admirable. The sequencing is catastrophic.

When you start with the destination, everything becomes a cost. Carbon neutrality is expensive. Certifications are expensive. Reporting is expensive. Every step is money flowing out with no clear mechanism for money flowing back in.

And when the money only flows out, sustainability doesn't survive. Not because the leadership didn't care. Because the economics didn't support it. Virtue without operations is just talk, and talk doesn't survive the next budget review.

The vast majority of small and mid-sized businesses set sustainability goals. Only a tiny fraction achieve measurable results. That's not a commitment gap. It's a systems failure. And the sustainability industry — the consultants, the certifiers, the software vendors — has a perverse incentive to keep it complicated, because the more complicated they make it, the more you need them.

A fifty-thousand-euro sustainability report that nobody reads is not a sustainability strategy. It's a receipt.

---

So here's what I started asking: what if you flipped the whole thing?

What if instead of starting with the goal and working backward, you started with your operations and worked forward? What if the same practices that reduce your environmental footprint also reduced your costs? What if the data you need for compliance also showed you where you're bleeding money? What if sustainability wasn't something you bolted onto marketing but something you built into how the business actually runs?

That's not hypothetical. That's what I've been building for the last ten years. First as agriculture-specific tools through Ecosystems United — compliance systems, ecolabel certification, supplier readiness for SMEs. After a decade I realized I'd been solving the same problem in every sector: businesses needed sustainability to work like an operating system, not a side project. I'd built the vertical first. The universal framework was hiding inside it.

I call it the Five Stacks Framework — and it works differently from everything you've been sold before.

Old sustainability gave you a report. Modern sustainability gives you a margin improvement.

Old sustainability started with ambition. Modern sustainability starts with operations.

Old sustainability was a cost center that competed with your actual business for attention and budget. Modern sustainability *is* your actual business — running smarter, leaner, and producing environmental proof as a byproduct of doing so.

The core idea is simple enough to fit on a napkin: **sustainability is the output, not the objective. The objective is a stronger business.**

When you build operations that are more efficient, more resilient, and more profitable, the environmental improvements come as evidence that the system is working. Not as the reason you built it. The output, not the input.

Same environmental outcomes. Fundamentally different economics.

---

I wrote a whole book about this — *The Modern Sustainability Playbook* — and made it free because the ideas need to spread faster than a paywall allows. You can grab it at [the5stacks.com](https://the5stacks.com). It lays out a five-stack framework, built in sequence, where each layer funds the next. Measurement funds efficiency. Efficiency reveals margin. Margin funds resilience. Resilience enables compounding.

But the starting point — the reason I'm writing this newsletter — is that we need to be honest about what went wrong. Not so we can be cynical about sustainability, but so we can stop repeating the same broken pattern and build something that actually holds.

The ambition was never the problem. The infrastructure was.

The question is whether we're willing to build differently this time — or whether we'll just keep announcing targets we have no operational plan to hit.

What would change in your business if sustainability was something that paid for itself instead of something you paid for?
