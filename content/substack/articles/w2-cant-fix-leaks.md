# You Can't Fix Leaks You Can't See

Here's a scene that plays out thousands of times a year across Europe.

It's a Thursday afternoon. You're deep in something operational — a production issue, a supplier negotiation, the normal chaos of running a business. An email arrives from one of your biggest customers. It's a sustainability questionnaire. Scope 1 emissions. Scope 2 emissions. Scope 3 estimates. Energy consumption by source. Waste by type and destination. Water usage. Material sourcing. Carbon intensity per unit of output.

It's due Friday.

You don't have the numbers. Not really. You have utility bills somewhere. Your waste hauler sends invoices, but you've never broken down the volumes by category. You have a rough sense of your energy consumption, but not by process — just the total bill. As for Scope 3, you're not entirely sure what that covers.

So you do what everyone does. You estimate. You round. You pull numbers from memory and hope they're close enough. You fill in the questionnaire and send it back, vaguely anxious that someone might ask how you calculated any of it.

Nobody asks. This time.

But the questionnaires are getting longer. They're arriving more often. And increasingly, your answers aren't just going into a file — they're being scored, compared, and used to decide whether you keep the contract.

Not visibility as in marketing. Visibility as in: can you actually see what's happening inside your own operation?

Most businesses can't. Not because they're negligent. Because nobody ever gave them a reason to look that closely. Utility bills come in, they get paid, they go in a folder. Waste gets hauled away, the invoice gets processed, done. Material comes in one end, product goes out the other, and what happens in between is understood in the heads of the people running the processes but not captured anywhere that would survive an audit.

This is normal. And it was fine — until it wasn't.

---

There are four reasons the baseline matters, and they compound on each other.

**First: you can't fix what you can't see.** This sounds obvious, but think about what it means in practice. If you don't know how much energy each process in your facility consumes, you can't know which process to optimize first. If you don't know your waste outputs by type and volume, you can't calculate what that waste is actually costing you — let alone whether any of it has recovery value. Every efficiency opportunity, every margin improvement, every cost reduction in your operation is invisible until you have the data to see it. The baseline is the map. Without it, you're fixing leaks in the dark.

Most SMEs have 15-30% waste hidden in their operations that nobody is tracking. Not because anyone is careless, but because nobody had the data to see it. Compressed air leaking from aging systems. Lighting running in unoccupied spaces. Overproduction that becomes scrap. Water flowing longer than it needs to. These aren't dramatic failures. They're slow drains that compound into serious money over a year. You cannot address what you have not measured.

**Second: your supply chain partners are already asking.** Large companies facing CSRD reporting requirements need Scope 3 data — emissions from their entire value chain. That means they need numbers from every significant supplier, distributor, and service provider in their chain. That's you. This is accelerating faster than most SMEs expect. The questionnaire that used to be optional is becoming a prerequisite for keeping the contract. Having your baseline ready when the request arrives isn't just compliance — it's a competitive advantage over the supplier who has to scramble.

Think about that from the buyer's perspective. Two suppliers, similar price, similar quality. One sends a link to their verified baseline data within the hour. The other says they'll need a couple of weeks to pull the numbers together. Which one do you trust more? Which one is easier to work with? That difference is showing up in procurement decisions right now.

**Third: the data that satisfies regulators is the same data that reveals where you're bleeding money.** This is the part that changes everything. The baseline isn't just a compliance exercise you endure. The data you collect to answer the questionnaire — energy by process, waste by type, material flows, water consumption — is the exact same data that shows you where your operation is inefficient. The same numbers that go into a CSRD report also tell you that Process B uses 40% more energy per unit than Process A. That your waste disposal costs have increased 22% year-over-year without anyone noticing. That you're paying for water that goes down a drain after a single use when it could be recirculated.

Compliance and operational intelligence are the same data set, viewed through different lenses. Build the baseline for one and you get both.

**Fourth: defensible means documented.** In a market increasingly skeptical of sustainability claims, "we think our emissions are around..." doesn't hold up. Not to auditors. Not to buyers. Not to regulators. Defensible means your methodology is recorded, your data sources are traceable, and your numbers can withstand the question: "How did you calculate that?"

This isn't bureaucratic overhead. It's credibility. "We reduced our emissions by 18%" means nothing without a verified starting point. The baseline is your proof — proof that your claims are real, proof that your improvements are measured against something solid, proof that distinguishes you from every competitor making unsubstantiated green claims on their website.

---

So how do you actually start?

The most common mistake is over-engineering it. Companies spend months evaluating sustainability software platforms, designing elaborate data architectures, building perfect systems — before collecting a single data point. Don't do this.

You need a structured tracker and the discipline to fill it in monthly. That's it to start.

Map what you already have. You're probably tracking more than you realize. Utility bills track energy. Waste hauler invoices track volumes and costs. Purchase orders track material inputs. Before you build new systems, inventory what's already sitting in your filing cabinet and your accounting software.

Pick the five to eight metrics that matter most for your sector. A food manufacturer's priorities look different from a logistics company's. Focus on the metrics your buyers are most likely to ask for, that your sector's regulations prioritize, and that will be most useful when you start looking for efficiency improvements.

Set up a monthly rhythm. Two hours a month is enough. Block the time. Collect the data. Enter it. Do this for three months and you have a baseline. Do it for twelve months and you have trend data that reveals patterns no single snapshot could show.

And document your methodology. For each metric, write down: what you're measuring, how you're measuring it, where the data comes from, and any assumptions you're making. This takes ten minutes per metric and saves you hours when someone asks you to justify a number.

Here's what changes once you have the data.

Your energy consumption stops being a bill and becomes a map — you can see which processes are draining the most, which shifts use the most power, where the patterns are. Your waste stops being a disposal cost and becomes a catalog of specific streams, some of which turn out to have value. Your material inputs stop being purchase orders and start revealing yield rates, inefficiency patterns, and sourcing risks.

The baseline isn't the end. It's the starting gun. Everything that follows in the Five Stacks — efficiency improvements, margin recovery, resilience planning, compounding returns — depends on this foundation. You can't optimize what you can't measure. You can't defend what you haven't documented. And you can't fix leaks you can't see.

So here's the question that matters: if you got that sustainability questionnaire tomorrow — the detailed one, the one that actually matters for your biggest contract — could you answer it? Not with estimates and apologies, but with real numbers and a documented methodology?

If not, that's your starting point. And it's a more valuable one than you think.
