## Week 1

**Movement:** 1 — "Here's what actually happened"

**Medium Title:** Is Sustainability As We Know It Really Sustainable?

**Substack Title:** Is Sustainability As We Know It Really Sustainable?

**Subtitle:** The sustainability industry is worth tens of billions. It's not clear it's changing anything.

**Tags/Keywords:** sustainability, SME, small business, carbon credits, greenwashing, sustainability industry, ESG, compliance, modern sustainability

**Writing Summary:** Open with the question: is the system sustainable? Not the planet — the system for addressing it. The sustainability industry is worth tens of billions. Companies buy credits, earn certificates, publish reports. Emissions still rise. Supply chains stay opaque. Trace the history: environmental concern → academic formalisation → corporate adoption (because there was money in looking good) → government mandates. By the time it reached someone running a real business, it was designed for companies with compliance departments. It's not about sustainability anymore. It's about exchanging capital for environmental outcomes. Buy a credit. Get a certificate. If you can't afford to play, you're invisible. That doesn't mean the environment doesn't matter. It means the system for protecting it was built without you in the room. End with quiet confidence: "The question isn't whether sustainability matters. It's whether there's a version of it that was built for you."

**LinkedIn (visual):** Timeline infographic — academic → corporate → government → you're holding the bill. Business voice: "Sustainability went through 3 transformations before it reached your business. None of them asked what you needed."

**Bluesky bites:**
- "Is sustainability as we know it really sustainable? Or is it just a market that sustains itself?"
- "Carbon credits are the indulgences of the 21st century. Pay for absolution, change nothing."
- "The sustainability industry is worth €50B. Most of it is spent explaining sustainability to people who already understand waste."

**Visual assets needed:**
- Timeline graphic: academic → corporate → government → SME left out (LinkedIn)
- Quote card: "Is sustainability as we know it really sustainable?" (Bluesky)
- Medium/Substack header image

## Week 2

**Movement:** 1 — "Here's what actually happened"

**Medium Title:** The Real Cost of Supply Chain Sustainability — And Who's Actually Paying It

**Substack Title:** Big Business Pushed Sustainability Downhill

**Subtitle:** You're not being asked to be sustainable. You're being asked to do their reporting.

**Tags/Keywords:** supply chain sustainability, sustainability questionnaire, ESG reporting, SME, scope 3, cost-shifting, supply chain engagement, compliance burden

**Writing Summary:** Open with the questionnaire. The largest companies made sustainability commitments. Then they needed to prove it. So they sent questionnaires downstream. Not designed for your business — designed for theirs, repackaged with your name at the top. Ten-dollar words for questions that boil down to: how much energy do you use, what do you throw away, where does your water go. You're not being asked to be sustainable. You're being asked to do big business's reporting for them. Figure it out. Fill it in. Or we won't choose you next time. That's not partnership. That's cost-shifting. The companies with the biggest sustainability budgets are outsourcing the hardest part — the actual data collection, the operational work — to the companies with the smallest ones. And calling it "supply chain engagement." End with the breadcrumb: "The data they're asking you to report? You're already sitting on most of it. You just never had a reason to look at it that way."

## Week 3

**Movement:** 1 — "Here's what actually happened"

**Medium Title:** Environmental Health Matters — The Delivery System Doesn't Work

**Substack Title:** This Is Not an Argument Against the Environment

**Subtitle:** The planet is in trouble. The system for addressing it wasn't built for your business.

**Tags/Keywords:** environmental health, climate change, sustainability approach, SME, small business sustainability, modern sustainability, operations, environmental outcomes

**Writing Summary:** Open with clarity. Climate change is real. Resource depletion is real. Pollution kills people. None of that is in question. What's in question is the approach. The current model says: care about the planet by buying credits, hiring consultants, and filing reports. What if environmental outcomes came from running a better business instead? Track your resources, stop leaking money, turn waste into revenue, build resilience. The environmental outcomes follow — not as a side project, not as a cost, but as a natural result of operations that actually work. The sustainability industry made environmental health into something you buy. We're saying it's something you build. This piece must be unapologetic about the environment mattering AND about the delivery system being broken. Both things are true. End with: "Environmental outcomes don't have to come from buying compliance. They can come from running a better business."

**LinkedIn (visual):** Side-by-side image: "Buying sustainability" (credits, certificates, consultants, reports) vs. "Building sustainability" (tracking resources, cutting waste, turning waste into revenue, proving it). Business voice: "There are two ways to approach environmental responsibility. One costs you money. The other saves it."

**Bluesky bites:**
- "Environmental health matters. The delivery system is broken. Both things are true."
- "The planet doesn't care whether you bought a certificate or actually reduced your waste. Only one of those changes anything."
- "Sustainability shouldn't be something you buy. It should be something you build."

**Visual assets needed:**

## Week 4

**Movement:** 2 — "There IS a better way"

**Medium Title:** Modern Sustainability Isn't About the Planet — It's About Your Operations

**Substack Title:** Modern Sustainability — What It Actually Looks Like

**Subtitle:** Sustainability is the output, not the objective. The objective is a stronger business.

**Tags/Keywords:** modern sustainability, operations, SME, business improvement, sustainability framework, efficiency, waste reduction, business case for sustainability

**Writing Summary:** This is the pivot week. The bridge from "here's what's broken" to "here's what works." Open with the premise: modern sustainability starts with a different question. Not "how do we become sustainable?" but "how do we run a stronger business?" When you track your resources, you find waste. When you fix waste, you save money. When you save money, you can invest. When you invest in resilience, you survive disruption. When you can prove all of this, you unlock financing, contracts, and market position. That's not idealism. That's a sequence. Each step pays for the next. No outside funding required. No consultant. No certification as a prerequisite. Start with what you have. This is the first time the reader sees the full sequence laid out. Don't use the word "stack." Don't number the layers. Just show the logic. End with: "It starts with seeing what's actually happening in your business. Not what a questionnaire asks about — what's actually there."

**LinkedIn (visual):** Carousel (8 slides): "The Modern Sustainability Sequence" — one slide per step in plain language. See what you have → find what you're losing → stop losing it → turn waste into value → protect against disruption → prove it to the market. Business voice: "Sustainability that works for real businesses follows a sequence. Each step funds the next."

**Bluesky bites:**
- "Sustainability is the output, not the objective. The objective is a stronger business."
- "Modern sustainability: track your stuff, stop wasting money, turn waste into revenue, prove it. That's the whole framework."
- "You don't need a sustainability strategy. You need better operations. The sustainability happens."

**Visual assets needed:**

**Substack Title:** See What You Actually Have

**Subtitle:** Your invoices already contain 80% of your environmental baseline. The other 20% takes an afternoon.

**Tags/Keywords:** environmental baseline, SME data, energy tracking, waste tracking, sustainability data, visibility, business operations, resource tracking

**Writing Summary:** The first practical week. Most SMEs don't know their real numbers — not because they're careless, because nobody ever gave them a reason to track this stuff in a useful way. Your electricity bill tells you what you owe, not where the energy goes. Your waste pickup tells you the cost, not the composition. Your supplier list tells you who, not how concentrated your risk is. The first step isn't action — it's visibility. What do you actually use? What do you throw away? What comes in, what goes out, what does it cost? You can get 80% of this from paperwork you already have. The other 20% takes an afternoon. And once you can see it, everything changes — because you'll immediately spot things you've been paying for without questioning. Soft-mention the baseline tool: "We built a free tool for exactly this." End with: "Once you can see what's actually happening, you can't unsee it. And you'll wonder how you ran this long without looking."

## Week 7

**Movement:** 2 — "There IS a better way"

**Medium Title:** Someone Will Pay You for What You're Throwing Away

**Substack Title:** That Waste Isn't Waste

**Subtitle:** Your output is someone else's input. The only question is whether you're part of that exchange.

**Tags/Keywords:** circular economy, waste to revenue, industrial symbiosis, NISP, waste exchange, SME, circularity, waste management, resource exchange

**Writing Summary:** You've tracked your resources. You've found the leaks. Now look at what you're throwing away. That offcut material? Another manufacturer needs it as input. That food waste? A composting company or biogas facility will take it. That packaging waste you're paying to dispose of? A recycler will collect it — sometimes for free, sometimes they'll pay you. Don't call it circularity. Call it turning a cost line into a revenue line. The UK's National Industrial Symbiosis Programme proved this works: 15,000 companies, £1 billion in cost savings, 47 million tonnes of waste diverted. Not theory. Not a pilot. At scale. The principle is simple: your output is someone else's input. You just need to find each other. Give concrete examples by sector — tire rubber → playground surfacing, fabric scraps → insulation, food waste → biogas. End with: "The stuff leaving your business as waste is entering someone else's business as a purchase. The only question is whether you're part of that exchange or paying to be excluded from it."

**LinkedIn (visual):** Carousel (5-6 slides): "Your waste is someone else's raw material" — one example per slide by sector. Business voice: "Jim's tire shop pays €3,200/year to dispose of rubber waste. A playground surfacing company 40km away buys the same material."

**Bluesky bites:**
- "Circular economy is just a fancy way of saying: stop paying to throw away stuff someone else would buy."
- "15,000 companies. £1 billion saved. 47 million tonnes of waste diverted. That's not a pilot programme. That's proof."
- "Your waste disposal company is the only one profiting from your waste. That's a market failure you can fix."

**Visual assets needed:**
- "Your waste → their input" flow diagram with sector examples (LinkedIn carousel)
- NISP stats graphic (shareable)

## Week 8

**Movement:** 2 — "There IS a better way"

**Medium Title:** The Risk You're Not Managing — Because Nobody Told You It Was There

**Substack Title:** Know What Breaks You

**Subtitle:** Most SMEs have a single point of failure they've never mapped. An afternoon with a whiteboard fixes that.

**Tags/Keywords:** business resilience, supply chain risk, concentration risk, single point of failure, risk management, SME, business continuity, supplier diversification

**Writing Summary:** You know your suppliers. But do you know what happens if one disappears? If your main raw material doubles in price? If a regulation changes overnight? Most SMEs have concentration risk they've never mapped. One supplier for a critical input. One customer that's 40% of revenue. One material with no substitute. That's not a business plan — that's a hope that nothing goes wrong. Resilience isn't a buzzword. It's knowing what breaks you before it breaks you. Map your dependencies. Identify your single points of failure. Ask: if this disappeared tomorrow, what would I do? You don't need a consultant. You need an honest afternoon with a whiteboard. Give a practical exercise: list your top 3 suppliers, top 3 customers, top 3 materials. If any one is >30% of your total, that's a concentration risk. End with: "Resilience isn't about preventing every disruption. It's about knowing which ones would kill you — and having a plan for those."

**LinkedIn (visual):** Image: "The single point of failure test" — diagnostic template. List your top 3 suppliers, customers, materials. Mark any >30% concentration. Business voice: "Every business has a point of failure they've never mapped. Usually it's the supplier they've had so long they stopped thinking about it."

**Bluesky bites:**
- "Resilience = knowing what kills your business before it happens. Most SMEs have never asked the question."
- "If one supplier disappearing would shut you down for 2 weeks, that's not a supplier relationship — that's a dependency."
- "An afternoon with a whiteboard mapping your dependencies is worth more than any risk assessment a consultant could sell you."

**Visual assets needed:**
- Single-point-of-failure diagnostic template (LinkedIn)
