# What You Discard Is Lost Margin

Let me tell you about a programme most people have never heard of.

Between 2005 and 2016, the UK ran something called NISP -- the National Industrial Symbiosis Programme. The idea was simple. Get companies in a room together. Map what each one throws away. Map what each one needs as input. Find the overlaps.

The results were staggering:

- One billion pounds in new sales generated
- 1.1 billion pounds in cost reductions, largely for SMEs
- 45 million tonnes diverted from landfill
- 47 million tonnes of CO2 reduced

All independently verified. All from the simple act of asking: "Does anyone need what you're throwing away?"

Then the government defunded it. The facilitated workshop model was too expensive to run -- human facilitators, venue costs, regional coordinators. It never became commercially self-sustaining.

And nobody built the digital replacement.

That fact still stops me in my tracks. Over two billion pounds in documented value for small and mid-sized businesses, a proven model, verified results. Gone. Replaced by fragmented regional efforts and a handful of platforms most SMEs have never heard of.

## The reframe

Here's the thing about waste: we've been thinking about it wrong.

Every waste stream in your operation has two costs. The obvious one is disposal -- what you pay to make it go away. The hidden one is the value inside it. Material that could be someone else's input. Heat that could warm another process. Equipment capacity sitting idle. Byproducts with market value that you're literally paying to throw into a landfill.

When you flip that frame, the numbers look different.

"You're paying 8,200 euros per year to dispose of something with recovery value."

That's not a sustainability talking point. That's a line item your CFO should be circling in red.

In the Five Stacks framework, Stack 3 is "keep more money." Stack 2 (efficiency) stopped the leaks. Stack 3 captures the value that's still walking out the door. The sustainability label for this is "circular economy." The business label is simpler: margin recovery.

## What margin recovery actually looks like

After you've run your operation through a proper efficiency lens (Stack 2), there are still outputs leaving your business that aren't products. Scrap material. Processing residues. Packaging waste. Organic matter. Heat. Water.

In the old model, every one of these is a cost. Disposal fees, hauling contracts, landfill charges. In the modern sustainability model, they're unrealized margin.

And it's not limited to physical waste. Equipment running below capacity is lost margin. Staff skills you never deploy are lost margin. Relationships with nearby businesses that could absorb your byproducts but were never explored -- lost margin.

The question for every significant waste stream is blunt: "Who would pay for this? Or at least take it for free so I stop paying to dispose of it?"

The answer, more often than you'd expect, is someone nearby.

Your processing residue might be another company's raw material. Your organic waste might have value as animal feed, composting input, or biomass fuel. Your scrap material might be sellable to recyclers at prices that exceed what you're paying to throw it away.

And every euro recovered this way hits your bottom line almost entirely as profit. Unlike revenue growth -- which comes with production costs, marketing costs, sales costs -- margin recovered from waste drops straight to the P&L. Disposal cost avoided plus recovery revenue earned. Pure margin improvement.

## The models that exist (and what they're missing)

After NISP collapsed, a few interesting models emerged across Europe. Each one solved part of the problem. None solved all of it.

**Sfridoo** in Italy built the country's first B2B industrial waste marketplace. Real transactions. Real SMEs in manufacturing clusters finding buyers for their waste. They prove it works in a Southern European context with family businesses and industrial districts. But it's regional, and discovery depends on listings rather than intelligence.

**FLOOW2** in the Netherlands built a B2B asset sharing marketplace with 35,000 users. But their most interesting feature is something almost nobody talks about: a tool called the SharingScan. It calculates the financial and sustainability benefits of joining the platform *before you commit*. It's the only pre-commitment financial calculator in the entire circularity landscape. It answers the question every business owner asks first: "What's this actually worth to me?" Nobody else has built that.

**Excess Materials Exchange**, also Dutch, went the AI route. Their platform matches waste streams with buyers using intelligent classification. Their framing is right -- "transform waste into revenue" -- and they focus on high-value reuse over commodity recycling. But it leans enterprise and comes with a consultant layer that prices out most SMEs.

Each of these is interesting. Each is also incomplete. Regional reach, enterprise pricing, or missing the financial pre-commitment calculation that SMEs need before they'll move.

The pattern across all of them: the thinking is right, the technology exists, but nobody has assembled it for the businesses that need it most.

## Why this is where modern sustainability gets exciting

Stacks 1 and 2 are internal. You're looking at your own data, finding your own leaks, fixing your own operations. Important work, but it's you working on your business alone.

Stack 3 is where the walls come down.

One company's waste becomes another company's input. A manufacturer's scrap metal becomes a smaller fabricator's raw material. A food processor's organic residue becomes a farm's compost. A factory's excess heat warms the greenhouse next door.

This is industrial symbiosis, and when it works, the economics are extraordinary. You save disposal costs. The other company saves input costs. Both operations become less dependent on volatile commodity markets. Both produce less waste. Both generate proof of circular practice that satisfies regulators and buyers.

It's the point where "sustainability is the output, not the objective" stops being a philosophy and starts being visible in inter-company transactions.

The longest-running example is Kalundborg Symbiosis in Denmark -- twelve partners exchanging steam, water, gas, fly ash, sludge, and yeast since 1961. Still operating. Proof that these relationships compound over decades when built on real value exchange rather than grant funding or good intentions.

## The progression that works

Here's the sequence I keep coming back to for Stack 3, and it's the same sequence NISP proved and that the successful European platforms are converging on:

**Tool first.** Take your waste data from Stack 1. Flip the frame: here's what you're paying to throw away. Here's what it might be worth. Category-based pathway suggestions -- this material type is commonly resold, recycled, or composted. No marketplace yet. Just awareness and a clear financial picture.

**Network second.** Once enough businesses in a region or sector are seeing their own data this way, matching becomes possible. Your waste profile meets another company's input requirements. Proximity matters -- transport costs kill long-distance waste exchange. Start local. Start specific.

**Marketplace third.** Open matching at scale. Users opt in to make their waste streams visible. The platform suggests matches based on material type, volume, and proximity. Cross-border when it makes sense. Verified transaction history feeds back into Stack 5 proof -- circular credentials for tenders, certifications, and financing.

The key insight from NISP is that companies didn't know their waste had value until someone showed them. The facilitator's job was to reframe waste as a resource. That reframing is exactly what a tool can do digitally -- at a fraction of the cost, for every SME that needs it, not just the ones lucky enough to attend a workshop.

## The disposal cost trend line

Here's the part that makes this urgent rather than interesting.

Landfill taxes, incineration fees, specialized waste handling charges -- the direction is up across Europe and most global markets. What costs you 50 euros per ton today will cost more next year. And the year after. The regulatory trajectory is clear: disposing of waste is going to keep getting more expensive.

Every waste stream you convert to a resource exchange is a stream you've removed from that escalating cost curve. Permanently. And every material you recirculate into your own process is a material you don't have to buy at market price -- insulating you from commodity volatility.

This isn't environmental virtue. It's captured revenue and structural cost reduction. It's the practical expression of modern sustainability: run a smarter operation, and the environmental proof falls out as a byproduct.

## The question nobody's answering

Seventy percent of manufacturers expect circular solutions to increase revenue by 2027. Forty-five percent haven't scaled a single circular initiative.

The gap isn't motivation. Business owners can see the logic of circularity. The gap is visibility -- the inability to see the financial case before committing. That's why FLOOW2's SharingScan concept is so important. It answers "what's this worth?" before asking "are you in?"

So here's what I keep turning over: we have proven models, documented results in the billions, existing technology, and rising disposal costs creating urgency. We have SMEs that want to act but can't see the financial case. We have a two-billion-pound programme that the UK government built, proved, and then let die.

What would happen if someone actually assembled all of this -- the NISP matching logic, the FLOOW2 financial calculator, the Excess Materials Exchange AI, the Sfridoo community trust -- into one platform that an SME could use for the price of a software subscription?

That's not a rhetorical question. I genuinely want to know what you think would happen.
