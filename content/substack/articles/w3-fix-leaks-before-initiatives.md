# Fix Leaks Before Adding Initiatives

Here's something nobody in sustainability wants to admit: the biggest environmental win most businesses will ever make has nothing to do with sustainability.

It's efficiency. Plain, boring, unsexy efficiency. Finding the money leaking out of your operation and stopping it from leaving.

I spent years watching companies skip this step. They'd measure their carbon footprint (expensive), set a net-zero target (ambitious), maybe buy some offsets (questionable), and announce the whole thing with a press release. Meanwhile, their compressed air systems were leaking 25% of their energy into the ceiling, and nobody had looked at the waste disposal invoice in three years.

They were funding the marketing department before they'd fixed the plumbing.

## The measurement trap

Every carbon accounting tool on the market will give you a number. "Your emissions are 142 tCO2e." Great. Now what?

That's where they stop. Seriously. The entire SME sustainability software market is built around measurement. Calculate your footprint. Get your number. Generate a report. Repeat next year.

Nobody builds what comes after.

The tools measure. They don't fix. They tell you what your emissions are but not where you're losing money, not which process is eating 30% more energy than it should, not which waste stream is costing you thousands a year for disposal when someone down the road would take it off your hands.

Measurement without action is an expensive hobby.

## What "save money" actually means

In the Five Stacks framework, I talk about each stack in terms of money, because that's the language that survives a budget review. The five stacks, in money terms:

| Stack | Money Frame | What It Does |
|-------|------------|--------------|
| 1 -- Metrics | **See your money** | Know where it goes, what flows through |
| 2 -- Efficiency | **Save money** | Stop the leaks |
| 3 -- Circularity | **Keep more money** | Capture value leaving your system |
| 4 -- Resilience | **Protect your money** | Don't lose what you've built |
| 5 -- Regeneration | **Find more money** | Unlock financing, premiums, contracts |

Stack 2 is "save money." Not save the planet. Not reduce your carbon intensity per unit of revenue. Save money. The sustainability happens as a byproduct.

Here's the flow: your baseline data from Stack 1 shows you where money is going. Stack 2 surfaces the patterns you've never noticed. It's the moment where someone looks at their own numbers and says, *"I had no idea I was losing money there."*

That reaction isn't hypothetical. It's the most common reaction.

## What the data actually reveals

Most SMEs have 15-30% waste hidden in their operations. Not because anyone is negligent -- because nobody had the data resolution to see it. When you start looking at your own patterns with the right lens, the surprises come fast:

- "Your energy cost per employee went up 23% in six months."
- "Site B costs 2.3 times what Site A costs per square meter."
- "One waste stream accounts for 60% of your disposal costs."
- "Your material costs as a percentage of revenue jumped from 29% to 34%."

None of this requires a sustainability consultant. It requires looking at your own data in a way that nobody has shown you before. Internal discovery first. Benchmarks second. Always expressed in euros, not tonnes.

The pattern is always the same: see yourself clearly, compare to context where it's available, decide what to act on, track what you did. That's it. No complexity. No jargon. Just your own numbers telling you a story you hadn't heard.

## The enterprise gap

Here's where it gets interesting. The right conceptual model for this already exists. A company called Carbmee built exactly what I'm describing -- efficiency-as-carbon-reduction, using something called MACC curves (marginal abatement cost curves -- basically, a ranked list of actions from cheapest to most expensive, measured by carbon reduced per euro spent). It's brilliant. It connects operational efficiency directly to emissions reduction and shows you the financial case for each action.

One problem: it's enterprise-only. Starting prices above 50,000 euros a year.

The SME market -- the 99% of European businesses that actually need this -- gets nothing. No MACC curves. No efficiency-as-carbon-reduction. No action layer on top of their measurement data. They get a carbon number and a PDF.

This is the gap that keeps surprising me. The thinking exists. The models exist. The math exists. But nobody has made it accessible to a company with 40 employees and a real budget constraint.

## Efficiency isn't a sustainability initiative

This is the part that matters most, and the part that most sustainability professionals get uncomfortable with.

Efficiency isn't a sustainability initiative. It's good business.

When energy prices spike, the company using 25% more energy per unit than it needs feels the hit 25% harder. When supply chains tighten, the company wasting materials has less buffer. When disposal costs rise -- and they are rising, everywhere -- the company that never looked at its waste invoices bleeds faster.

Typical energy efficiency improvements deliver 10-25% cost reduction. Water efficiency measures regularly achieve 15-35% consumption reduction. Material waste reduction translates directly to margin improvement. Payback periods for basic measures are often under twelve months. Many are under six.

And the environmental proof? It falls out automatically. When you use less energy, your emissions go down. When you waste less material, your resource footprint shrinks. When you optimize your processes, every environmental metric improves. You didn't set a sustainability target. You ran a tighter operation. The sustainability is the output.

## Where most people go wrong

The mistakes are predictable because I've seen them repeated across industries:

**Technology before basics.** Companies invest in IoT sensors and monitoring platforms before fixing the compressed air leak that's costing them hundreds a month. Fix the leak first. Buy the sensor later.

**Marginal focus over material focus.** Spending weeks optimizing a process that accounts for 2% of your energy while ignoring the one that accounts for 30%. Work the list from top to bottom. Biggest numbers first.

**The efficiency blitz.** Running a one-time efficiency project, capturing the easy wins, then stopping. Efficiency isn't a project. It's a culture. Build it into monthly reviews. Track it. Make it visible. The culture matters more than any single initiative.

**Ignoring the people who see it daily.** Your floor staff -- machine operators, maintenance teams, warehouse workers -- see inefficiency that no audit will find. They know which machines run when they shouldn't. They've always wondered why that pump runs overnight. Ask them.

## The flywheel starts here

Efficiency isn't exciting. It doesn't photograph well for your annual report. Nobody wins awards for fixing compressed air leaks.

But it does something more important: it funds everything that comes next. The savings from Stack 2 create budget headroom for circularity, resilience, and market positioning. You're not asking the board for new sustainability budget. You're reinvesting money the system already found.

Stack 1 gave you the map. Stack 2 is the first return on building that map. And it sets up something even more interesting: as you track and optimize your resource flows, waste streams with hidden value become visible. That's Stack 3 -- but that's next week's conversation.

For now, here's the question: if you've already measured your baseline, what did you actually do with the data? And if you didn't do anything with it -- why did you measure in the first place?
