# How a 300-Year-Old German Word Became a $30 Billion Industry

*Sustainability used to mean "don't cut more trees than grow back." Now it means something very different.*

---

The word sustainability is German. It's over 300 years old. And it had nothing to do with environmentalism.

Let's talk about where this actually came from — and how it became a $30 billion industry that somehow still can't tell Jim, who owns three tire shops, what he's supposed to do with that questionnaire his biggest customer just sent him.

## Before the Industry

For most of human history, sustainability wasn't a concept. It was just how you survived.

**Indigenous land management** — every continent, thousands of years. Controlled burns to prevent wildfires and regenerate soil. Rotational farming so land could recover. Seasonal harvesting so fish stocks could replenish. Nobody called it sustainability. They called it "eating next year."

**Edo period Japan (1603-1868)** — 250 years of near-total isolation. An island nation with finite resources and no trade. Nothing was wasted because nothing could be replaced. Clothes were repaired until they became rags. Rags became paper. Paper became fuel. An entire economy built on "use everything." Not because they were environmentalists. Because they had no choice.

**Medieval European commons** — shared grazing lands, forestry rules, water rights. Communities set limits on how much anyone could take. Not because they were green. Because overharvesting destroyed everyone's livelihood. You graze too many sheep, the land dies, everyone starves. Self-interest enforced restraint.

**Hans Carl von Carlowitz, 1713** — and here's where the word itself comes from. A Saxon mining administrator in Germany. The mines needed timber — lots of it — for shoring up tunnels and fuelling smelters. They were running out of trees.

He wrote *Silvicultura oeconomica* and coined the word "Nachhaltigkeit" — sustainability. His argument was simple: don't cut more than grows back, or you destroy the resource that feeds your industry.

That's not environmentalism. That's operational resource management. That's a language Jim understands.

**The key point:** for most of human history, sustainability was just good management. It became something else entirely when industrialisation broke the feedback loops that kept extraction in check.

## Industrialisation Breaks It

Fossil fuels removed the natural limits. Suddenly you could extract faster than systems could replenish. You could produce more than markets could absorb. You could pollute more than ecosystems could process.

Scale changed everything. One factory pollutes a river — that's a local problem. A thousand factories pollute a continent — that's a systemic one.

And the costs got externalised. The company profits, the community downstream gets sick. The factory produces cheap goods, the atmosphere absorbs the emissions. The shareholders gain, the public pays.

For 200 years, this was just called "progress."

## The Rediscovery

### 1960s-70s: The Science

Rachel Carson published *Silent Spring* in 1962. The Cuyahoga River in Ohio caught fire in 1969. People could physically see the damage — dead birds, burning rivers, smog you could taste.

The response was regulation. Clean Air Act. Clean Water Act. The EPA. The approach was straightforward: stop poisoning things. And for the visible stuff — the rivers, the air, the obvious pollution — it largely worked.

### 1980s-90s: The Corporate Response

Companies realised regulation was coming whether they liked it or not. Better to self-regulate than be regulated. "Corporate Social Responsibility" emerged — voluntary reporting, sustainability statements, environmental pledges.

The Brundtland Report in 1987 put "sustainable development" into the global vocabulary: *"development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."*

This is where sustainability starts its transformation. It stops being a practice and starts becoming an industry.

### 2000s: The Frameworks

The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI). The UN Global Compact. CDP launches in 2000. Reporting becomes standardised. Which means it becomes a product.

Companies hire sustainability teams. But here's the thing — most of those teams aren't hired to change operations. They're hired to fill out reports. The gap opens between what companies report and what they actually do.

The reporting becomes an end in itself.

### 2010s: The Acceleration

ESG becomes an investment lens. BlackRock's Larry Fink writes his annual letters telling CEOs that sustainability matters to investors. Rating agencies emerge: EcoVadis, Sustainalytics, MSCI ESG.

Supply chain questionnaires cascade. Big companies, under pressure to report on their entire value chain, push reporting requirements down to their suppliers. Paris Agreement (2015). The Sustainable Development Goals. The language becomes universal.

Everyone's talking about sustainability. The question is whether anyone's actually doing it.

### 2020s: The Industry

CSRD. EU Taxonomy. SFDR. Mandatory reporting arrives in Europe. What was voluntary becomes legal requirement.

The sustainability industry is now worth tens of billions. Software platforms. Consulting firms. Auditing services. Certification bodies. Rating agencies. Training programmes. Conference circuits.

And at the bottom of the chain? Jim. With a 47-page questionnaire from his biggest customer. No sustainability team. No budget. A deadline. And the vague sense that if he doesn't figure this out, he loses the contract.

## The Lenses

Here's what makes sustainability so confusing: different groups see it completely differently, and they're all talking past each other.

**Academia** sees research, models, peer review. Important work. Also largely disconnected from the reality of running a 15-person business. When a professor publishes a paper on Scope 3 methodology, it doesn't help Svenja figure out what to put in the form her customer sent her.

**The haters** — "ESG is woke." "Sustainability kills shareholder value." "It's all a scam." Often funded by industries that benefit from no regulation. But here's the uncomfortable truth: they have a point when they say the current system is performative. They just draw the wrong conclusion. The answer isn't to abandon environmental responsibility. The answer is to fix the delivery system.

**The corporate world** sees sustainability as reporting plus compliance plus brand positioning. A department, not a practice. Something you do in your annual report, not something that changes how you run your factory.

**The alphabet soup** — ESG, CSR, CSRD, CDP, GRI, SASB, TCFD, SFDR, VSME. Every framework adds a layer. Each layer needs software, consultants, auditors. Nobody asks whether Jim needs all of them. They just keep adding letters.

And since someone will ask — here's what the scopes mean in plain language:

- **Scope 1:** What comes out of your building and vehicles. Your direct emissions. The gas you burn, the diesel in your trucks.
- **Scope 2:** Your electricity. Indirect emissions from the power grid.
- **Scope 3:** Everything else. Your entire supply chain. Your customers' use of your product. This is where it gets genuinely absurd for a small business.

## The Question That Matters

For 300 years, sustainability meant "manage your resources so your business survives." Hans Carl von Carlowitz would recognise Jim's tire shop as a perfectly sustainable operation — Jim manages his resources, serves his customers, employs his people, and plans to pass the business to his daughter.

Somewhere along the way, sustainability stopped meaning that. It became a compliance industry worth tens of billions of euros.

The question is: who does that industry actually serve?
